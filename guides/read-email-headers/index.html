How to read email headers to spot phishing

Where to find full email headers in Outlook, Gmail and Microsoft 365, how to read Received lines, SPF, DKIM, DMARC and compauth results, and the red flags of spoofing and business email compromise.

Updated 2026-11-03 · 10 min read

A user forwards an email: "Our bank details have changed, please update before Friday's payment." The display name is a real supplier. The logo is right. The user wants to know if it's legitimate, and they want to know now. The body won't tell you; it was written to look genuine. The email headers will, because most of them are written by the mail servers along the way, not by the sender.

This is how to read email headers to spot phishing and spoofing, in the order that gets you to an answer fastest.

Read the Received lines from the bottom up

Every server that handles the message adds a Received: line at the top. So the bottom Received line is the oldest hop, closest to the sender, and the top one is your own server.

Received: from mail.badsender.top (91.215.85.142) by AM9PR02MB7012.mail.protection.outlook.com ...
Received: from DESKTOP-7H2K (unknown [45.88.97.11]) by mail.badsender.top (Postfix) ...
  • In from X (Y [IP]), X is the name the sender claimed and is easily forged. The IP in brackets is what the receiving server actually saw on the connection. Trust that one.
  • The first public IP in the chain is usually the true origin. Lines below your organisation's first server can be forged by the sender, so treat them as hints.
  • Large gaps between hop timestamps or times going backwards suggest forged lines or a queued spam run.

Authentication results

Your receiving server records its verdicts in Authentication-Results (and Microsoft adds compauth):

Authentication-Results: spf=fail (sender IP is 91.215.85.142) smtp.mailfrom=badsender.top;
 dkim=none (message not signed) header.d=none; dmarc=fail action=quarantine header.from=contoso-finance.com; compauth=fail reason=000
CheckWhat it provesPitfall
SPFThe sending IP is allowed by the envelope domain (Return-Path)Checks the hidden envelope domain, not the From address you see
DKIMThe message was signed by domain d= and not changed sinceAny domain can sign; what matters is which domain
DMARCSPF or DKIM passed and lined up with the visible From domaindmarc=pass is the result that actually protects the From address
compauthMicrosoft's combined verdict, including implicit authenticationfail means Microsoft thinks the From domain is spoofed

Rule of thumb: dmarc=fail on a domain you recognise is the strongest single spoofing signal. dmarc=pass only proves the domain is real, not that it is the company you think it is: check for lookalike domains such as rnicrosoft.com or contoso-finance.com.

Sender fields and BEC red flags

  • Reply-To differs from From: replies go to the attacker. The classic business email compromise setup, especially with invoice or payment requests.
  • Display name tricks: "Microsoft 365 Billing" <billing@random-domain.top>, or a display name that contains a different email address.
  • Return-Path on an unrelated domain without DMARC pass.
  • Message-ID created by a server unrelated to the claimed sender.
  • X-Mailer values like PHPMailer or scripts in mail that claims to be from a large company.
  • Spam verdicts: X-MS-Exchange-Organization-SCL of 5 or more, or SFV:SPM in X-Forefront-Antispam-Report.

One trap catches even experienced analysts: a message that passes everything. If a supplier's real mailbox has been compromised, SPF, DKIM and DMARC all pass because the email genuinely came from them. The tells are behavioural instead: an unusual request, a new Reply-To, a sending IP in a country the supplier doesn't operate in, or an X-Mailer that doesn't match their usual mail client. When money is involved, confirm by phone on a number you already had.

A five-minute triage checklist

  1. Get the full headers or the .eml.
  2. Read DMARC and compauth first. Fail on a known brand: treat as phishing.
  3. Compare From, Reply-To and Return-Path domains.
  4. Find the originating IP (first public IP in the brackets) and check its reputation.
  5. Look at the links' real destinations and any attachment hashes, never by clicking.
  6. If it is phishing: purge similar messages from all mailboxes, block the sender domain and IPs, and check whether anyone clicked or replied.

FAQ

Can email headers be faked?

Partly. A sender can add fake Received lines and set any From, Reply-To or display name. What they can't fake are the lines your own mail servers add, including the connecting IP and your server's Authentication-Results. That's why you read from your own server downwards and trust only what it recorded.

If DMARC passes, is the email safe?

No. DMARC pass proves the From domain is authentic, nothing more. The domain could be a lookalike registered last week, or a real supplier whose mailbox was taken over. Check the domain spelling and the request itself.

Why does SPF pass but DMARC fail?

SPF checks the hidden envelope sender (Return-Path), which can be any domain the attacker controls. DMARC also requires that domain to match the visible From address. A pass on a different domain doesn't count, which is exactly the trick DMARC exists to catch.

Where's the sender's real IP address?

In the oldest Received line your organisation's servers added, inside the brackets after the claimed hostname. Microsoft 365 sometimes adds X-Originating-IP for mail sent through its own clients.
