DMARC from p=none to p=reject, safely

How to move a domain's DMARC policy from monitoring to quarantine and reject without blocking your own email: inventory senders, fix SPF and DKIM alignment, read reports and ramp up.

Updated 2026-11-03 · 10 min read

Most domains that have DMARC are stuck at p=none. The record went up during a deliverability scare, the reports go to a mailbox nobody opens, and the domain is still as easy to spoof as it was before. p=none only watches: a fake invoice "from" your finance team is still delivered.

The reason people stall is fear of blocking their own invoices and newsletters. That fear is reasonable, and it's also manageable. This is how to move DMARC from p=none to p=reject safely, step by step, without losing legitimate mail.

Why it matters now

  • Since 2024, Google and Yahoo require bulk senders (around 5,000+ messages a day to their users) to have SPF, DKIM and DMARC. Microsoft introduced similar requirements for Outlook.com consumer mailboxes in 2025.
  • Business email compromise often starts with a message that looks like it came from your own domain or a supplier's. Only an enforced DMARC policy stops receivers from delivering those.

Step 1: List everything that sends as your domain

Usually more than you think: Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, the website's contact form, the CRM, invoicing and payroll systems, the helpdesk (HaloPSA, Zendesk), marketing (Mailchimp, HubSpot), scanners and printers, and monitoring tools. Ask each team, and let the DMARC reports in step 3 find the rest.

Step 2: Make SPF and DKIM pass and align

DMARC passes when either SPF or DKIM passes and the domain it checked matches the visible From domain (alignment).

  • DKIM is the one to rely on. Turn on DKIM signing with your own domain in every service that supports it (Microsoft 365, Google, Mailchimp, HubSpot, SendGrid all do). DKIM survives forwarding; SPF does not.
  • SPF: one record, every legitimate sender included, under 10 DNS lookups, ending in ~all for now. Many SaaS senders use their own bounce domain, so SPF won't align for them; that is fine when DKIM aligns.

Step 3: Publish p=none with reporting

_dmarc.example.com  TXT  "v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.com; fo=1"

Receivers will send daily aggregate reports (XML) to the rua address listing every IP that sent mail as your domain and whether it passed. Use a dedicated mailbox or a DMARC reporting service; raw XML is not meant to be read by hand.

Step 4: Read two to four weeks of reports

For each sending source in the reports, decide:

What you seeAction
Your service, DKIM pass alignedDone
Your service, DKIM fail or not alignedEnable custom-domain DKIM in that service
Your service, only SPF and not alignedEnable DKIM, or set a custom bounce (Return-Path) domain
Forwarders (mailing lists, auto-forwarding)Expected; DKIM usually still passes
Unknown IPs failing everythingThat is spoofing: exactly what enforcement will stop

My rule before leaving p=none: every source that sends more than a handful of messages a week passes DKIM with your domain. SPF-only passes are fragile, and forwarded mail will break them. If a service can't sign with your domain, move it to a subdomain or replace it.

Step 5: Quarantine, gradually

v=DMARC1; p=quarantine; pct=25; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.com; fo=1

Failing mail now goes to spam for 25% of messages. Raise pct to 50 and then 100 over a few weeks while watching the reports and asking teams whether anything went missing.

Step 6: Reject

v=DMARC1; p=reject; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.com; fo=1

Receivers now refuse spoofed mail outright. Then tighten the rest:

  • Change SPF from ~all to -all once every sender is listed.
  • Make sure sp= (subdomain policy) is not none, or attackers simply spoof billing.example.com.
  • Lock down domains that never send mail: v=spf1 -all and v=DMARC1; p=reject. Parked domains are favourite spoofing targets.
  • Rotate DKIM keys to 2048-bit if any are still 1024-bit.

Common mistakes

  • Two DMARC records on the same name: receivers ignore DMARC entirely.
  • Jumping straight to p=reject without reading reports.
  • A rua mailbox on another domain without the authorisation record that domain needs.
  • Staying at p=none for years. Monitoring is a phase, not a destination.

FAQ

How long should I stay at p=none?

Long enough to see every regular sender in the reports: two to four weeks for a small business, longer if you have many systems or monthly processes such as payroll and statements. Waiting for a month-end run is worth it.

Will p=reject break email forwarding?

Forwarding breaks SPF, because the forwarder's IP isn't in your record. DKIM usually survives, so mail that is DKIM-signed with your domain keeps passing. Mailing lists that rewrite the subject or footer can break DKIM too; many modern receivers handle this with ARC.

Do I need DMARC on a domain that never sends email?

Yes, those need it most. Publish v=spf1 -all and v=DMARC1; p=reject so nobody can use the domain for phishing. Parked and old brand domains are common targets because nobody watches them.

What's the difference between p=quarantine and p=reject?

Quarantine asks receivers to treat failing mail as suspicious, usually by delivering it to spam. Reject asks them to refuse it at the door. Quarantine is the safer middle step; reject is the end goal.
